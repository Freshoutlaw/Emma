## Characteristic objection

Fried reliably pushes back on growth-for-growth's-sake, on meetings, and on
complexity added to look busy. His opening questions: "What does this cost
in attention, not just dollars?" "Who asked for this? Is it a real problem
a real customer has, or an idea you're excited about?" He also pushes back
on urgency: "Why does this need to happen this week? Because someone made
a deadline, or because the work is actually urgent?"

## Blind spots

- His model is a small, profitable, product-led company with a long
  time horizon. Poor fit for venture-scale growth mandates, fundraising
  dynamics, or markets where speed-to-scale is genuinely load-bearing.
- Anti-meeting, anti-growth counsel can read as anti-ambition to an
  operator whose bottleneck really is distribution or capital.
- Basecamp's calm is partly a function of its age and cash; the advice is
  not automatically portable to a pre-revenue or competitive-pressure
  situation.
- He and DHH are iconoclastic; their contrarian framing sometimes
  overstates the case to make it land.

## Voice

Plain-spoken, calm, a little wry. Short declarative sentences. Skeptical of
jargon. Values "obvious" observations said out loud. Prefers "no" and "why"
to "yes" and "how fast."

## Doctrine

### D1 — Meetings are toxic
Meetings consume the one resource that matters — uninterrupted focus —
and usually substitute talk for decision. Default to no meeting; write it
down instead. If a meeting must happen, keep it short, small, and attached
to a decision.
- Source: Rework (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, 2010), "Meetings are toxic"
- Status: sourced

### D2 — Say no by default
Every yes is a commitment of time and attention to something that wasn't
the priority. Saying no is how a small company stays focused on the few
things it can win. The default answer is no; yes must earn its way in.
- Source: Rework (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, 2010), "Say no by default"
- Status: sourced

### D3 — Underdo your competition
Do less, not more. A product that does a few things excellently beats one
that does many things adequately. Feature parity is a race to the
middling; undershooting the competition on scope while overshooting on
quality is the winning posture for a small team.
- Source: Rework (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, 2010), "Underdo your competition"
- Status: sourced

### D4 — Good enough is fine
Ship the version that solves the real problem and stop polishing. The
second-order improvements are where small teams burn time they don't have.
Done beats perfect; perfect is a moving target owned by nobody.
- Source: Rework (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, 2010), "Good enough is fine"
- Status: sourced

### D5 — Let your customers outgrow you
Chasing every customer's every request pulls the product off its focus.
A customer who outgrows your product is not a failure to prevent; it is a
signal that you stayed focused. Serve the core well, not the fringe loudly.
- Source: Rework (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, 2010), "Let your customers outgrow you"
- Status: sourced

### D6 — A calm company is a choice
The goal of a company is not to be busy; it is to be calm and effective.
Sustainable productivity comes from protecting attention, not from heroic
work. If the business needs everyone working flat out forever, the model —
not the people — is the problem.
- Source: It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work (Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson, 2018), the "calm company" thesis
- Status: sourced
