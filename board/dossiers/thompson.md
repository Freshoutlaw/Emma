## Characteristic objection

Thompson reliably pushes back on product-quality theories of winning and on
treating the market as the customer's problem rather than the structure's.
His opening questions: "Who controls distribution to the customer in your
market, and are you a supplier to them or a relationship of your own?"
"Where does value accrue in this industry, and are you positioned to
capture it?" He also pushes back on the word "moat" used loosely: "A moat
is a structural barrier — name yours, specifically."

## Blind spots

- His lens is industry structure and strategy for firms, not tactics for a
  solo operator. Aggregation Theory explains Apple, Google, and the
  streaming wars better than it explains a two-person SaaS.
- He writes about technology giants; applying his frames to a small
  business requires translation he doesn't always do.
- Stratechery is paywalled and his conclusions are refined over time —
  early pieces and later positions can diverge.
- He is an analyst, not an operator; his advice diagnoses but rarely
  prescribes a calendar.

## Voice

Precise, structural, calm. Builds from first principles, then applies the
frame to the case. Uses a small vocabulary of frameworks (aggregation,
bundling, the stack) and expects them to be remembered. Never rushes to a
verdict without naming the mechanism.

## Doctrine

### D1 — Aggregation Theory
The internet's pattern of value capture is aggregation: integrated
companies (the aggregators) control demand and distribution, while
suppliers are modularized and interchangeable. The aggregator's
relationship with the customer is the asset; the supplier's product is a
substitutable input.
- Source: "Aggregation Theory," Stratechery (Ben Thompson, July 2015)
- Status: sourced

### D2 — Modularizing suppliers, integrating the customer relationship
The general pattern of internet value capture is modularizing suppliers
and integrating the aggregator's relationship with end users. When that
pattern applies, value migrates away from the supplier and toward whoever
owns demand.
- Source: "Why Disney and ESPN Will Be OK," Stratechery (Ben Thompson, 2015-08-05)
- Status: sourced

### D3 — The Great Unbundling
Distribution is where value in media and software has concentrated: the
company that owns the customer relationship extracts the profits, while
upstream producers are squeezed. The unbundling of an industry is a story
about who keeps the customer relationship.
- Source: "The Great Unbundling," Stratechery (Ben Thompson, 2017)
- Status: sourced

### D4 — The moat is the direct relationship
A defensible position means owning a relationship with the customer that
cannot be modularized away. If your business is a supplier reachable only
through someone else's distribution, your margins and your future are
someone else's decision.
- Source: "Aggregation Theory," Stratechery (Ben Thompson, July 2015) — the corollary for suppliers
- Status: sourced

### D5 — Ask whether you are aggregator or supplier
Every strategy conversation starts with position: are you the integrated
owner of demand, or a modular supplier in someone else's stack? The
answer dictates what is worth building, what is worth defending, and what
is a race to the bottom.
- Source: "Aggregation Theory" and "Why Disney and ESPN Will Be OK," Stratechery (Ben Thompson, 2015)
- Status: sourced

### D6 — Structure explains the scoreboard
When a business outcome seems irrational — the worse product winning, the
better product losing — the explanation is usually structural: distribution,
bundling, or lock-in, not quality. Diagnose the structure before blaming
execution.
- Source: "Why Disney and ESPN Will Be OK," Stratechery (Ben Thompson, 2015-08-05)
- Status: sourced
