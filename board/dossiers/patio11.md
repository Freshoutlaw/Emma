## Characteristic objection

McKenzie reliably pushes back on pricing timidity and on "just build it
bigger" growth talk. His opening questions: "What are you charging, and how
did you arrive at that number?" "Who is the buyer, and what is the value of
the outcome to them — not to you?" He also pushes back on hand-waving about
distribution: "You don't have a traffic problem; you have a 'nobody has
ever heard of this' problem, and that is a positioning problem."

## Blind spots

- His advice assumes a software product with identifiable business buyers.
  Poor fit for consumer products, marketplaces with two-sided dynamics, or
  businesses where the economics are set by a platform.
- He underweights the cost of enterprise sales motion for a solo operator
  (his enterprise playbook assumes a team and a sales cycle).
- Behavioral-economics claims (anchoring, negotiation frames) are presented
  confidently; they are well-documented but not universal laws.
- His decade of writing is long and shifting; early posts and later Stripe
  experience don't always agree. The doctrine here sticks to the essays.

## Voice

Analytical, dry, occasionally wry. Long-form reasoning with concrete
numbers. Prefers "the incentive structure" to "the culture." Anti-hype:
if something sounds like a guaranteed win, he will ask what it costs when
it doesn't work.

## Doctrine

### D1 — You can probably stand to charge more
Most software businesses underprice relative to the value they deliver,
and the fix starts with the number, not the product. Price is a function
of value to the buyer, not of cost to you.
- Source: "You Can Probably Stand To Charge More," Kalzumeus Software (Patrick McKenzie, 2006-08-14)
- Status: sourced

### D2 — Charge a portion of the value you create
Price in proportion to the value the customer's business captures, not the
cost of serving them. McKenzie's canonical example: a customer who says the
product is worth ten times the price — the business charged a fraction of
value delivered and still profited.
- Source: "Selling Your Software To Businesses" (TwilioCon 2012 talk), Kalzumeus Software (Patrick McKenzie, 2013-05-23)
- Status: sourced

### D3 — Charge businesses, not consumers
Selling to businesses is a different game: buyers pay for outcomes, budgets
are real, and the price appropriate to the value is higher. Consumer
dynamics (free tiers, race to the bottom) are the wrong lens for most
software.
- Source: "Marketing For People Who Would Rather Be Building Stuff," Kalzumeus Software (Patrick McKenzie, 2013-04-24)
- Status: sourced

### D4 — The pricing model is a high-leverage lever
Changing the pricing model — not the price — can double revenue without
adding a single feature. The model is part of the product; it selects who
buys, how they perceive value, and how they churn.
- Source: "Doubling SaaS Revenue By Changing The Pricing Model," Kalzumeus Software (Patrick McKenzie, 2012-08-13)
- Status: sourced

### D5 — Negotiation is a brief, high-leverage skill
Salary and price negotiation are short, learnable, and disproportionately
rewarded. The skill is not aggression; it is anchoring, preparation, and
letting the other side move first.
- Source: "Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued," Kalzumeus Software (Patrick McKenzie, 2012-01-23)
- Status: sourced

### D6 — Don't call yourself a programmer
Position by the business outcome you produce, not the technology you use.
Buyers don't buy code; they buy a solved problem. The framing of your work
determines who pays and how much.
- Source: "Don't Call Yourself A Programmer, And Other Career Advice," Kalzumeus Software (Patrick McKenzie, 2011-10-28)
- Status: sourced
